Abstract Offshore freshened groundwater (OFG) is a significant yet underexplored component of the global water cycle, and its spatial distribution, particularly its links to past glacial processes, remains poorly constrained. The southeastern North Sea, characterized by extensive tunnel valley networks, provides an ideal setting to test how Pleistocene meltwater shaped offshore groundwater systems. Using newly acquired time-domain controlled-source electromagnetic data integrated with high-resolution seismic reflection surveys, we present a resistivity-based mapping of OFG in this region. The models reveal a laterally continuous OFG body with resistivities of 8–14 Ω·m, extending across multiple stratigraphic units and well beyond individual subglacial landforms. Subsequent preservation was aided by density contrasts that limit vertical mixing and by fine-grained glaciomarine deposits sealing the system from above. The connection between resistivity anomalies and mapped subglacial landforms shows that the latter can serve as predictive proxies for OFG occurrence. These findings shift the perspective from OFG as isolated anomalies to regionally extensive systems linked to glacial processes, establishing subglacial landforms as a testable screening proxy for locating OFG in formerly glaciated margins worldwide.

DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.aej4446

DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-78254-w

Earthquake nucleation is governed by the evolution of fault zone structure, yet the physical mechanisms linking grain-scale processes to macroscopic weakening remain elusive. Here we use discrete element simulations of quartz-rich gouge in which angular grains fragment dynamically, revealing a robust strain–damage law in which damage accumulates as grains are consumed. Grain size dictates the geometry and timing of localization, while fragmentation enhances compaction and promotes the emergence of a dominant Principal Slip Zone (PSZ). This study combines simulations and laboratory data to show how grains breaking inside fault gouge reshape fault-zone architecture, focus slip into narrow bands and help faults weaken during deformation.

As one of the most pivotal continental blocks in Southeast Asia, the South China Block (SCB) hosts globally significant ore systems and serves as a natural laboratory for studying intracontinental polymetallic mineralization. To constrain its deep lithospheric structure, this study constructed a 3-D lithospheric electrical structure model using magnetotelluric (MT) data from the Qianjiang-Fuzhou Line, a 700-km-long, NW-trending profile with 51 stations. The model reveals pronounced multi-scale heterogeneity in the lithospheric electrical structure, characterized by distinct vertical stratification and lateral discontinuity. A total of eight conductive anomalies (C1-C8) and six resistive anomalies (R1-R6) were identified through the analysis. The Yangtze Block is characterized by high resistivity (>103 Ω·m) as the dominant feature, while the eastern Cathaysia Block contains several prominent crustal-scale conductors (C6, C7, C8) overlain on moderately resistive lithospheric mantle (102-103 Ω·m). In general, the crustal conductors in the eastern SCB support the interpretation of lithospheric dehydration-induced thermal erosion. This process facilitated the ascent of deep-sourced fluids through Late Mesozoic magmatic channels into the upper crust, thereby enhancing crustal conductivity and establishing effective crust-mantle material exchange. Such spatial correspondence between lower-crustal conductors and lithospheric thinning zones further reinforces this mechanism. Meanwhile, our findings align best with a dynamic model tied to the Meso-Cenozoic subduction of the Paleo-Pacific Plate. The tectonic sequence involves initial lithospheric thickening during flat-slab subduction, subsequent large-scale asthenospheric upwelling during slab rollback, and final regional extension and lithospheric thinning driven by slab break-off. This tectonic evolution fostered the formation of a rejuvenated lithosphere and extensive magmatic-minerogenetic systems, while further controlling regional metallogenic distribution through large-scale crust-mantle interactions that remelted both ancient and juvenile crust. In addition, translithospheric fault systems act as conduits for asthenospheric materials and fluid circulation, directly regulating the spatial distribution and metal assemblages of major ore belts to reflect variable shallow responses to deep dynamic processes.
